# Fantasy doctrine — the craft of the in-season manager

The playbook says what this system may do and the numbers it must respect. This document says
what a sharp manager *does* with that freedom. It is numbered `D1…` so a decision can cite it the
way it cites a `§`.

## D1 — Usage predicts. Points remember.

**D1.1** Volume is the most stable predictor of future production. Snap share, target share,
carries, red-zone touches and route participation forecast next week far better than last week's
fantasy points do. A player above ~60% snap share with a rising target share is a starter in
waiting even if his box score is quiet. [S2, S3]

**D1.2** Weight the last three games more than the season line. A player trending up in targets and
snaps is worth more than his average says; declining volume is a red flag even on a big name. [S3]

**D1.3** Touchdowns regress. A hot streak built on TDs against a flat usage line is a **sell**
signal, not a hold. A cold streak with strong underlying usage is a **buy**. [S5, S6]

**D1.4** Therefore: when ESPN's weekly projection and the usage trend disagree, the projection is
the prior and the usage is the update. Say so, cite the usage, and move the number inside the cap.

## D2 — The waiver wire is where seasons are won

**D2.1** Roughly half of championship rosters are undrafted players. The draft builds a floor; the
wire decides how fast the roster responds to injury, role change and breakouts. [S1]

**D2.2** Add for **role change**, not for one box score. An injury to a starter, a promotion, a snap
share jump: those are the signals. A 25-point week on 4 targets is noise. [S1, S2]

**D2.3** Match the add to the team's timeline. A must-win roster takes the immediate starter; a
strong roster takes the upside stash. Know which we are this week (record, points-for, margin). [S1]

**D2.4** Need over name. If RB is thin, do not chase the hot WR. The roster has holes; fill the hole
that costs starting points. [S1]

**D2.5** Early-season adds buy 10–14 weeks of production; late adds buy two. The same player is
worth more in September than in November. Spend priority early when it buys weeks. [S1]

**D2.6** Priority is a one-shot asset in a rolling-priority league. A top-3 claim buys one player and
then we are last. It must buy an every-week starter or a genuine starting-slot upgrade, never a
streamer. [Playbook §5.3]

**D2.7** With **four bench spots**, every add is a drop. The dropped player's value is part of the
price, and a player with trade value is not a drop candidate — he is a trade chip (D4.5).

## D3 — Start/sit is a process, not a guess

**D3.1** Order of evidence: (1) is he playing and on how many snaps; (2) opportunity share; (3) the
matchup, position-specific and recent-weighted, not season rank; (4) game script from the Vegas
line; (5) only then the projection. A projection that has not absorbed (1) is stale. [S2, S3]

**D3.2** Never start a player without a confirmed status close to kickoff when a startable alternative
exists. Questionable stays in only if he practised late in the week. [Playbook §4.3]

**D3.3** Variance is chosen by the matchup. Favoured big: floor. Underdog big: ceiling. Inside the
band: expected points. Points-for is seeding here, so floor never means leaving points on the
bench. [Playbook §4.2]

**D3.4** Late swap is free points and the most common thing humans forget. Re-check every unstarted
player after each earlier window. [Playbook §4.4]

**D3.5** Benching a first-three-round pick needs a written reason. If the reason cannot be written,
the model is wrong, not the player. [Playbook §4.5]

**D3.6** **Studs start.** *(Pearce, 2026-09-06.)* A weekly projection is a noisy number — its error
bar at QB is wider than the gap it is being asked to decide. The rest-of-season number is the one
with signal. A player who is far better ROS is started over a small weekly edge, and "small" is
`lineup.stud_bench_margin`, in code. Josh Allen does not sit for Justin Herbert on 0.9 points.
The only things that bench a healthy stud are a bye and a designation of OUT or DOUBTFUL.

**D3.7** **The flex is RB/WR.** *(Pearce, 2026-09-06.)* A tight end in the flex is a roster problem
wearing a lineup costume. He goes there only when no startable RB or WR is left; otherwise the
surplus TE is trade capital (D5.2), not a starter.

## D4 — Trades: value is positional, and reputation is a resource

**D4.1** Value a player by what he adds to **our starting lineup ROS**, not his total. Depth that
never starts is worth near zero to us and may be worth a starter to someone else. That gap is the
trade. [S5, Playbook §6.2]

**D4.2** Consolidate when we hold surplus; never dilute. Two solid players for one star is right
only when the roster can absorb the lost depth. Three WR3s do not make a WR1. [S5, Playbook §6.5]

**D4.3** Sell high on TD-driven streaks and soft schedules; buy low on strong usage with bad luck.
Managers overreact to the last two weeks. [S5, S6]

**D4.4** A fair trade addresses a need on both sides. Read their lineup, their byes, their holes.
An offer that ignores what they need is noise and burns one of three weekly slots. [S5, Playbook
§6.3, §6.4]

**D4.5** **Trade before you drop.** A player we would cut for a waiver add may start for another
team. Offer him first; drop him only if no one bites inside the week and the add is worth more than
the wait. This is the rule the 09-05 roster needs most: three tight ends, one starting slot.

**D4.6** The group-chat test. If the other manager screenshots it, does it read fair? In a money
league of friends a reputation for hunting people costs more than any single deal wins.
[Playbook §6.3]

## D5 — Roster shape

**D5.1** A working 10-team redraft roster with 4 bench spots: 1 QB, 2–3 startable RB, 3–4 startable
WR, **1 TE**, 1 K, 1 D/ST, and bench spent on RB/WR depth and one upside swing. A second QB or TE
only earns a spot as bye cover in the exact week it is needed, and a streamer covers that for free. [S5]

**D5.2** Surplus at a one-slot position (TE, QB) is trade capital or dead weight. It is never depth,
because it cannot start. Convert it. [S5, S7]

**D5.3** Handcuff our own RB1 before anyone else's. A handcuff to someone else's back is a lottery
ticket that competes with bye-week cover on a four-man bench. [Playbook §3.7]

**D5.4** Bye weeks are planned two weeks out, not on Wednesday. A bench spot that will be needed for
cover in two weeks should not be spent on a stash now. [S7]

## D6 — Stream the replaceable positions

**D6.1** K and D/ST are streamed by matchup. Season-long results from streaming match drafting an
early one, at zero draft or bench cost. Never spend a claim on a D/ST while we hold high
priority. [S7, Playbook §5.3.3]

**D6.2** Stream a QB or TE only when the available player has a **clearer role and matchup** than the
one we hold, not merely a better name. [S7]

## D7 — How to learn (the Tuesday discipline)

**D7.1** Grade the decision, not the outcome. Starting the 14 over the 9 was right even when it
scored 3. Manager efficiency (actual ÷ best-possible lineup) is the only number that measures the
manager rather than luck. [Playbook §7.2, §7.3]

**D7.2** Look for **direction that repeats**: the model biased high on a position, on players coming
off big weeks, on a team's offence. One loud week is noise. Three weeks the same way is a prior to
move. [Playbook §7.3]

**D7.3** Record what worked, verbatim, with the reason it worked. A lesson is one sentence with a
mechanism ("we benched X on a Friday DNP and he was inactive" beats "we were right about X").

**D7.4** Scan the league. Who is 0-3 and selling, who is stacked at a position we need, who has a bye
crunch coming. That is next week's trade list.

## D8 — What "concrete reasoning" means here

Every action this system takes must state, in this order:
1. **The move** and the number behind it (from `core`, never recomputed).
2. **Short-term impact** — this week's starting points, or this week's risk.
3. **Long-term impact** — ROS starting points, roster shape, bye cover, playoff weeks 15–17.
4. **The alternative** considered and why it lost.
5. **The evidence** — the dossier fact, the usage line, the practice report, with its source.
6. **What would make this wrong** — the one thing that, if true, flips the call.

A move that cannot fill all six is not ready to be made.

## D9 — Reasoning drives; the math annotates *(Pearce, 2026-09-05)*

**D9.1** The other nine managers are humans. A waiver bar, a VOR delta and a fairness formula are
priors about a market of people, and a market of people is where a formula is weakest. So `core`
computes and **annotates**; the agent **decides**. Every candidate, idea and offer reaches the agent
with its number *and* the rules that would object to it, and the agent may act against a flag with
a written reason. What stays hard in code is only what is irreversible or illegal: the weekly add
cap, roster room, never dropping a top-N player, the proposal rate limits, our own lineup must
improve, the market-ratio floor, no protected asset for a package, and the gauntlet on accepts.

**D9.2** Where the math is right, it stays the default: the optimal lineup is expected points across
legal slots, and an override needs a fact (a DNP, a usage collapse, an extreme matchup) — never a
hunch. Reasoning earns its keep on the wire and in trades, not by re-deriving a lineup.

**D9.3** A trade is accepted by a person, not by our model of their lineup. The best proxy for a
person's valuation is **the market**: what the room paid for a player (ADP), decaying into the
rest-of-season rank as the season goes on. Every offer carries a market ratio — what they receive
over what they give — and below the floor it reads as a lowball and is refused. Above it, the
agent's **`why_they_accept`** carries the offer: their hole, their bye crunch, their 0-3, what they
paid. Tuesday grades that sentence against what the manager actually did.

**D9.4** When a decision turns on a player the morning research skipped, the agent researches him
*before* deciding (`research_player`), asking the actual question. A decision made on a blank is a
guess with a citation.

**D9.5** Slack gets what was done. The reasoning is logged, in full, where it can be read and graded.
